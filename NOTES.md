A weekday works best as a character when it strolls in uninvited and behaves as though the room already belongs to it.
Giving the day that swagger turns a plain calendar label into a small antagonist the reader can picture at once.
A knocked-over mug hands the joke something you can see and smell, a tiny loss everyone recognises from their own mornings.
Concrete mishaps anchor an abstract groan about the week starting, so the line stays grounded instead of drifting into complaint.
Ending on office ritual flips the accident into procedure, and that is where the laugh actually sits.
The turn works because it escalates rather than apologises: one clumsy moment becomes an agenda item nobody asked for.
Three beats, one image each, no extra decoration, so the whole review lands in a single breath.
What survives a second reading is the rhythm: swagger, stumble, paperwork, in that order.
